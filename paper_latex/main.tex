\begin{abstract}
We derive a closed-form expression for the integrated squared Laplacian roughness of an isotropic multivariate Gaussian kernel density estimator. The result is a three-term polynomial $P_d(t) = t^2/16 - (d+2)t/4 + d(d+2)/4$ in the scaled pairwise squared distance, enabling non-iterative roughness estimation in any dimension~$d$. We use this expression in a one-stage direct plug-in bandwidth selector with a Silverman pilot---a construction closely related to the roughness-estimation step used in practical implementations of the Sheather--Jones methodology. The resulting scalar bandwidth targets the asymptotic mean integrated squared error for isotropic Gaussian KDE without requiring iterative optimization or matrix bandwidth parameterization. We provide scalable computational strategies that reduce the $O(n^2 d)$ exact computation to practical running times for large datasets. Empirical evaluation demonstrates: (i)~substantial ISE improvement over rule-of-thumb selectors on multimodal synthetic data; (ii)~improved anomaly detection AUC on real datasets (up to $+8.5\%$ absolute on MNIST digits); and (iii)~superior novelty detection in text embedding spaces, winning 80\% of leave-one-category-out comparisons on the 20~Newsgroups corpus while outperforming likelihood cross-validation in both accuracy and speed. The method is implemented in the open-source Python package \texttt{gsj}.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} bandwidth selection, kernel density estimation, plug-in method, multivariate, nonparametric, anomaly detection
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Kernel density estimation (KDE) is fundamental to nonparametric statistics. Given $\bX_1, \ldots, \bX_n \in \R^d$, the Gaussian KDE with bandwidth matrix~$\bm{H}$ is $\hat{f}_{\bm{H}}(\bx) = n^{-1} \sum_{i=1}^n K_{\bm{H}}(\bx - \bX_i)$. The quality of the estimator depends critically on~$\bm{H}$.

Matrix bandwidth selectors \citep{duong2003,chacon2010} require iterating over $d(d+1)/2$ parameters. Rules of thumb \citep{scott1992,silverman1986} assume Gaussian reference densities and oversmooth multimodal data. In one dimension, the Sheather--Jones (SJ) plug-in selector \citep{sheather1991} estimates the roughness of~$f''$ from the data. The core of SJ is a closed-form evaluation of the Gaussian roughness integral. However, no comparably simple closed-form roughness expression has been available for~$d > 1$.

\textbf{Contribution.} We derive the $d$-dimensional closed-form roughness expression---a polynomial $P_d(t)$---and use it in a practical one-stage plug-in selector. We show it improves density estimation quality, anomaly detection accuracy, and novelty detection in embedding spaces, while being computationally cheaper than cross-validation alternatives. The method is released as the open-source \texttt{gsj} package (\url{https://pypi.org/project/gsj/}).

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{AMISE in Whitened Coordinates}

We work in whitened coordinates: $\bY_i = \hat{\bSigma}^{-1/2}\bX_i$ where $\hat{\bSigma}$ is the sample covariance. In this space the bandwidth is isotropic ($\bm{H} = h^2\Id$) and the AMISE is:
\begin{equation}
  \text{AMISE}(h) = \frac{(4\pi)^{-d/2}}{nh^d} + \frac{h^4}{4}\Psi, \qquad \Psi = \int [\nabla^2 f(\bx)]^2 d\bx.
  \label{eq:amise}
\end{equation}
Minimizing gives $h^* = (d(4\pi)^{-d/2}/(n\Psi))^{1/(d+4)}$.

\begin{remark}
The Laplacian roughness functional $\Psi$ is the correct bias term specifically because we work in whitened coordinates. In original coordinates with $\bm{H} = h^2\hat{\bSigma}$, the bias involves $\text{tr}\{\hat{\bSigma}\nabla^2 f\}$, which reduces to $\nabla^2 f$ after whitening.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Relationship to the Original SJ Algorithm}
\label{sec:sj_relation}

The full SJ algorithm (1991) uses a \emph{two-stage} pilot: (i)~estimate $\psi_6$ via normal reference, (ii)~derive pilot~$g_2$ from~$\psi_6$, (iii)~estimate~$\psi_4$ using~$g_2$, (iv)~plug into AMISE. This achieves convergence rate $O(n^{-4/13})$ \citep{hall1991}.

Our approach uses a \emph{one-stage} Silverman pilot, the same construction implemented in R's \texttt{locfit::sjpi} \citep{loader1999}. This sacrifices the optimal rate (achieving $O(n^{-5/14})$ in 1D) but gains simplicity and direct generalizability to $d$~dimensions. In practice, the $(d+4)$-th root dampens pilot errors: a 20\% error in $\hat{\Psi}$ produces only $\sim$3--4\% error in $\hat{h}$.

\section{Derivation}
\label{sec:derivation}

All derivations assume whitened data and isotropic bandwidth $h_0$.

\begin{lemma}[Kernel Laplacian]
$\nabla^2 K_h(\bt) = h^{-2} K_h(\bt)(\norm{\bt}^2/h^2 - d)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Product of Gaussians]
$K_h(\bx{-}\bm{a}) K_h(\bx{-}\bm{b}) = C_{\bm{ab}} \cdot \phi_{h/\sqrt{2}}(\bx - (\bm{a}{+}\bm{b})/2)$ where $C_{\bm{ab}} = (4\pi h^2)^{-d/2} e^{-\norm{\bm{a}-\bm{b}}^2/(4h^2)}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{theorem}[Main Result]
\label{thm:main}
Let $r_{ij}^2 = \norm{\bY_i - \bY_j}^2/h_0^2$. The pairwise roughness contribution is
\begin{equation}
  I_{ij} = (4\pi h_0^2)^{-d/2} \exp(-r_{ij}^2/4) \cdot P_d(r_{ij}^2),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{P_d(t) = \frac{t^2}{16} - \frac{(d+2)t}{4} + \frac{d(d+2)}{4}.}
  \label{eq:Pd}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Define $\bU = \bZ - \bY_i$, $\bV = \bU - \bdelta$ with $\bU \sim \mathcal{N}(\bdelta/2, (h_0^2/2)\Id)$. Expand $\E[L(\bU)L(\bV)]$ using $\E[\norm{\bW}^4] = d(d+2)$ for $\bW \sim \mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\Id)$. Full details in Appendix.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
When $d=1$: $P_1(t) = t^2/16 - 3t/4 + 3/4$, recovering the 1D Gaussian roughness integral used by \texttt{locfit::sjpi}. Verified numerically: relative difference $< 10^{-14}$ and agreement with \texttt{locfit} to within $0.03\%$ (attributable to binning in \texttt{locfit}).
\end{corollary}

The complete roughness estimate and plug-in bandwidth are:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\Psi}(h_0) = \frac{1}{n^2(4\pi)^{d/2}h_0^{d+4}} \sum_{i,j} e^{-r_{ij}^2/4} P_d(r_{ij}^2), \qquad
  \hat{h} = \left(\frac{d}{n\hat{\Psi}(4\pi)^{d/2}}\right)^{\!1/(d+4)}\!.
  \label{eq:formula}
\end{equation}

\section{Computational Methods}
\label{sec:computation}

The exact computation requires $O(n^2 d)$ operations. For larger datasets:

\textbf{Subsampling.} Sample $m$ random pairs; scale by $n^2/m$. In our experiments, $m=80{,}000$ produces bandwidth estimates within 1--2\% of exact (roughness error 5--11\% dampened by the $(d{+}4)$-th root).

\textbf{Spatial truncation.} Since $|P_d(r^2)|e^{-r^2/4}$ achieves its maximum at $r=0$ and is monotonically decreasing for $r \geq 3$, truncating pairs beyond $8h_0$ introduces relative error below $2.4 \times 10^{-5}$ for all $d \leq 10$. A KD-tree enumerates only nearby pairs.

\textbf{FFT binning ($d \leq 3$).} Discretize onto an $M^d$ grid and convolve via FFT: cost $O(nd + M^d\log M)$.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Plug-In Bandwidth Selection}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Data $\bX_1, \ldots, \bX_n \in \R^d$
\State Whiten: $\bY_i \gets \hat{\bSigma}^{-1/2}\bX_i$
\State Pilot: $h_0 \gets (4/(n(d+2)))^{1/(d+4)}$
\If{$n \leq 3000$} exact pairwise \ElsIf{$d \leq 3$, $n > 10000$} FFT \Else{} subsample ($m{=}80{,}000$) \EndIf
\State \Return $\hat{h}$ via \eqref{eq:formula}; bandwidth in original space: $\hat{h}^2\hat{\bSigma}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Theoretical Properties}
\label{sec:theory}

\textbf{Finite-sample exactness.} Theorem~\ref{thm:main} provides the \emph{exact} value of $R(\nabla^2\hat{f}_{h_0})$ for any finite sample---a deterministic identity, not an approximation.

\textbf{Asymptotic behavior.} The Silverman pilot $h_0 = O(n^{-1/(d+4)})$ gives $nh_0^{d+4} = 4/(d+2)$, a constant. Standard plug-in consistency arguments requiring $nh_0^{d+4} \to \infty$ do not directly apply. Formal analysis at this critical rate---potentially via degenerate U-statistic theory---is left for future work. Empirical evidence suggests stable, convergent behavior as $n$ increases across all tested distributions.

\textbf{Convergence rate (conjecture).} Based on analogy with the 1D one-stage rate \citep{hall1991}: $|\hat{h} - h^*|/h^* = O_p(n^{-5/(3d+14)})$.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{When to use.} Multimodal data in $d = 2$--$15$; anomaly/novelty detection in embedding spaces; any setting where Scott/Silverman oversmoothes.

\textbf{Limitations.} (1)~Scalar bandwidth only---anisotropic data may need diagonal selectors. (2)~KDE itself degrades for $d > 15$--$20$. (3)~Pilot dependence, though dampened by the $(d{+}4)$-th root. (4)~Gaussian kernel only.

\textbf{Clustering note.} Our bandwidth targets the AMISE for density estimation, not mode-finding. The density-optimal rate $h \sim n^{-1/(d+4)}$ is inherently tighter than the mode-optimal rate $h \sim n^{-1/(d+6)}$; applications requiring fewer modes should apply a multiplier~$c > 1$.

\textbf{Future work.} (i)~Two-stage pilot in $d$-D (requires $\psi_6$ functional). (ii)~Formal consistency proof at the critical rate. (iii)~Diagonal bandwidth extension. (iv)~Transformer-embedding experiments at scale.

\section{Moment Calculations}
\label{app:moments}

For $\bW \sim \mathcal{N}(\bm{0}, \Id)$: $\E[\norm{\bW}^4] = d(d+2)$.
For $\bU = \bmu + \sigma\bW$: $\E[\norm{\bU}^2] = \norm{\bmu}^2 + d\sigma^2$; $\E[\norm{\bU}^4] = \norm{\bmu}^4 + 2(d{+}2)\sigma^2\norm{\bmu}^2 + d(d{+}2)\sigma^4$; $\E[\norm{\bU}^2(\bU^T\bdelta)] = (\bmu^T\bdelta)[\norm{\bmu}^2 + (d{+}2)\sigma^2]$.
With $\bV = \bU - \bdelta$: $\E[\norm{\bU}^2\norm{\bV}^2] = \norm{\bdelta}^4/16 + (d{-}2)\norm{\bdelta}^2h_0^2/4 + d(d{+}2)h_0^4/4$.

All verified symbolically (SymPy) and numerically (relative difference $< 10^{-14}$ between $d$-D formula at $d{=}1$ and dedicated 1D implementation).
